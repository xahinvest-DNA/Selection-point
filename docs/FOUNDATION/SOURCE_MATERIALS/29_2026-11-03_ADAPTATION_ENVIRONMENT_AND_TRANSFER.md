# Source Material 29 — Adaptation, simulated environment and transfer

**Date:** 3 November 2026  
**Status:** working source material; not approved Foundation canon  
**Origin:** owner insight + external evidence review  
**Purpose:** preserve the new product hypothesis without silently upgrading it into a universal scientific claim.

## 1. Owner insight

The owner proposed a strong reframing:

> A person cannot simply be “taught” a different way of being by transferring information. A more useful design model is to place the person in an environment to which the nervous system and behaviour can adapt through repeated experience.

The immediate Selection Point implication is that the project should stop treating explanation as the primary mechanism and should instead design repeated situations in which selection, action, consequence and feedback are experienced.

This statement is treated as a **product-design hypothesis**, not as a literal claim that learning through instruction is impossible in every domain.

## 2. Scientific correction to preserve

The project must not use the popular formulation:

> “The brain does not distinguish what happens in reality from what happens in imagination.”

Available evidence does not support that equivalence. Mental imagery, motor execution and working-memory processes show partial overlaps and important differences.

A safer working statement is:

> Simulated or imagined experience can recruit learning-relevant cognitive and motor processes and can improve performance in some tasks, but it is not equivalent to real execution and does not guarantee transfer to real behaviour.

## 3. Evidence anchors

### 3.1 Imagery practice in basketball

Shan, Yang & Wang (2026), systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis of basketball imagery practice:
- 14 study units / 39 effect sizes;
- positive overall estimate;
- clearest outcome evidence for free-throw performance;
- authors caution about small samples, clustered effects and possible small-study effects.

Reference:
- PMID 42601963
- DOI `10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1888190`

Project use: evidence that mental/simulated rehearsal can contribute to skill development in bounded tasks. Not evidence that imagery equals real practice or that all behavioural capacities can be trained this way.

### 3.2 Motor imagery is not simply covert movement

Van Caenegem et al. (2026), meta-analytic comparison across neuroimaging studies:
- motor imagery shared neural recruitment with both movement execution and working memory;
- analyses emphasized a substantial executive/working-memory component;
- this argues against treating imagery and physical execution as neurologically identical.

Reference:
- PMID 41537051
- DOI `10.1162/IMAG.a.1095`

Project use: preserve epistemic restraint; design simulation as cognitive/decision practice rather than a literal duplicate of real action.

### 3.3 Immersion is not the same as transfer

Hoyne et al. (2026), randomized study in Australian Rules Football:
- compared 360° VR, 2D video and control over four weeks;
- 2D video showed superior field-based decision-making transfer compared with 360° VR and control post-intervention;
- authors concluded that immersion was not the underpinning mechanism; relevant perceptual information was crucial.

Reference:
- PMID 42690462
- DOI `10.1007/s00426-026-02372-9`

Project use: Selection Point V0 does not need VR. Representative decision structure and meaningful information-action relations have priority over technological immersion.

## 4. Product translation

The evidence does not prove Selection Point. It supports a narrower design direction:

```text
repeated representative situation
-> perception of relevant structure
-> selection
-> consequence
-> feedback
-> varied repetition
```

The project should then test a separate transfer chain:

```text
simulated distinction
-> real event
-> actual realized continuation
-> consequence
-> evidence of transfer / non-transfer / contradiction
```

## 5. Design implications

1. Experience should precede or dominate explanation where possible.
2. A scenario should preserve the cues and constraints relevant to the target selection, not merely tell a story.
3. Repetition should vary surface conditions so that the participant cannot succeed by memorizing one answer.
4. The simulator should expose delayed and cumulative consequences where the real world normally hides them across time.
5. Project vocabulary is secondary to practical discrimination and action.
6. Performance inside the simulator is not mastery.
7. Transfer must be measured in reality.
8. High-tech immersion is optional; mechanism testing comes first.
