# 1. Executive synthesis

The current evidence supports a major refinement of the Stage 1 direction:

> **The practical problem is not automaticity itself. The practical problem is whether a currently cued automatic continuation remains aligned with reality and the relevant direction, and whether the system can interrupt and retrain it when it is not.**

This yields two separate training problems:

```text
A. INTERRUPTION / REOPENING
old automatic continuation is cued
→ create a usable interruption window
→ restore a relevant executable alternative

B. RE-AUTOMATIZATION
alternative is repeatedly realized
→ cue/response relation becomes easier and faster
→ cognitive load falls
→ useful response may become a new default in relevant contexts
```

The evidence does **not** support the naive model:

```text
strong intention
→ old habit disappears
→ new behavior follows
```

Intentions can be sincere and strong while behavior still fails to follow. In physical-activity meta-analytic work, a large proportion of intenders did not realize the intended behavior. Experimental work similarly shows that increasing intention produces a much smaller change in behavior than in intention itself.

A second major finding is that behavior change often does **not erase old learning**. Extinction/counterconditioning literatures commonly find new, context-sensitive learning layered over older associations. Return to old contexts, time, stress or loss of alternative reinforcement can permit old responses to reappear.

Therefore, a lapse is not sufficient evidence that the person's earlier choice was insincere or that no learning occurred.

A third finding is that useful automaticity is a legitimate endpoint. Repetition in stable contexts strengthens cue-response associations and reduces cognitive load. Skill-learning literature similarly treats automaticity as one consequence of practice. Expert naturalistic decision-making often relies on rapid recognition rather than exhaustive conscious comparison.

The strongest working hypothesis for SP is therefore:

> **Selection Point is not a permanent replacement for automaticity. It may be the temporary architecture by which an outdated automatic continuation is detected, interrupted, replaced, reality-tested and practiced until the more useful continuation itself becomes sufficiently automatic.**

This hypothesis requires empirical testing inside SP and must not yet be treated as established canon.

# 2. Evidence map — why intention is insufficient

## 2.1 Intention–behavior gap

**External evidence: strong / replicated at behavioral level, domain-limited.**

Relevant sources:
- Rhodes et al. physical activity intention-behavior meta-analysis, PMID 37460164;
- Rhodes & Dickau experimental meta-analysis, PMID 22390739;
- Yang et al. 2025 meta-analysis, PMID 40470022.

The consistent finding is not that intention is irrelevant, but that intention alone is an incomplete predictor of realization.

Working boundary:

```text
strong intention
≠
strong probability of execution under every cue/state/context
```

This directly supports RC-018.

## 2.2 Why this matters for SP

The current self-pilot observation that a person can make a sincere decision and later enter an old route is compatible with the broader intention-action literature.

However, SP must not infer one universal reason for the gap. Candidate contributors can include:
- cue-triggered response availability;
- state/load;
- context;
- missing alternative response;
- weak retrieval of the earlier plan;
- changed facts;
- reward structure;
- planning error;
- unknown.

No gap category is automatically `sabotage`.

# 3. What can interrupt an established automatic continuation?

No single universal method is supported. The literature instead suggests several intervention classes.

## IM-1 — Context / cue disruption

**Evidence status:** relatively strong for context dependence of habits; intervention transfer depends on domain.

Relevant evidence:
- Wood et al. `Changing circumstances, disrupting habits`, PMID 15982113;
- Bouton 2021 `Context, attention, and the switch between habit and goal-direction`, PMID 34713424;
- Bouton & related renewal/extinction work.

Key finding:

> When the usual performance context changes, habitual cueing can weaken and behavior can become more responsive to current intention/goal information.

SP implication:

```text
before relying on in-the-moment willpower,
ask whether the cueing environment can be changed
```

Examples of designer-side intervention classes:
- remove cue;
- change sequence;
- alter access/friction;
- move the behavior to a different context;
- place retrieval/support cue earlier than the old response.

Boundary: context change may create an opportunity but does not itself create the desired replacement behavior.

---

## IM-2 — Implementation intentions / if-then replacement

**Evidence status:** moderate-to-strong across multiple domains; effect size varies and breaking unhealthy habits appears harder than adding desired behavior.

Relevant evidence:
- Adriaanse et al. 2011, PMID 21317315;
- Gollwitzer-related implementation intention research, PMID 15647140;
- healthy eating meta-analyses, PMID 21056605 and 31125588;
- mental-health goal attainment meta-analysis, PMID 25965276.

A particularly relevant result is that counterhabitual implementation intentions can remove the cognitive accessibility advantage of the habitual response in the critical situation.

But they do not instantly make the alternative response automatic.

Working model:

```text
IF critical cue/state/context appears
THEN execute preselected alternative
```

SP implication:

The choice may need to be made **before** the cue arrives, so the future Selection Point is scaffolded by a prepared response.

Boundary:
- if-then planning is not proof of realization;
- alternative goal commitment still matters;
- effectiveness varies by behavior and context.

---

## IM-3 — Competing / substitute response

**Evidence status:** strong in specific Habit Reversal Training domains; generalization to all everyday SP behaviors is limited.

Relevant evidence:
- Woods & Miltenberger review, PMID 7593685;
- HRT clinical and applied studies in tics/BFRBs/speech;
- recent habit-degradation longitudinal work, PMID 41664447.

HRT commonly combines:

```text
awareness of cue/urge
+
response incompatible with the target response
```

SP implication:

Pure inhibition (`do not do X`) may often be weaker operationally than a pre-available alternative (`when X cue occurs, do Y instead`).

But this is a **candidate design principle**, not yet a universal SP rule.

---

## IM-4 — Response inhibition / stopping

**Evidence status:** real but modest and domain-specific.

Meta-analyses of inhibitory-control training for appetitive behavior report small-to-moderate behavioral effects, with substantial heterogeneity (e.g. PMID 39397386; earlier PMID 26592707).

SP implication:

A trained pause/stop response may be one tool for generating a brief open edge.

Boundary:

```text
stopping old response
≠
knowing what to do next
```

Therefore inhibition alone is unlikely to be a sufficient architecture.

---

## IM-5 — Awareness / present-moment contact

**Evidence status:** compatible but not sufficient by itself.

Habit-reversal work shows that awareness training can reduce some repetitive behaviors in specific settings. Mindfulness/urge-surfing studies suggest that changing the response to urge can sometimes affect behavior even without eliminating the urge.

SP implication:

This strongly fits the current capability:

> `notice enough of what is happening now` can be the entry condition for interruption.

But:

```text
awareness
≠
interruption
≠
replacement
≠
new automaticity
```

This prevents Selection Point from collapsing into mindfulness training.

---

## IM-6 — Reward/consequence restructuring

**Evidence status:** broad learning-theory support; specific SP implementation open.

Habit and learning literature consistently treats consequences/reinforcement as relevant to repetition and maintenance. Recent habit-degradation work found lower habit strength on days when the habitual behavior was not performed and when reward for reducing it was above average (PMID 41664447).

SP implication:

A new continuation that is repeatedly more costly, less available or less rewarding than the old one may be difficult to automate even if it is consciously preferred.

Designer question:

> What makes the new continuation practically repeatable, not only morally desirable?

# 4. The old route may persist

## 4.1 Extinction is often new learning, not deletion

**Evidence status:** strong in basic learning research; transfer to complex everyday human behavior must be cautious.

Relevant reviews:
- Bouton 2000, PMID 10709948;
- Bouton 2002, PMID 12437938;
- Bouton et al. 2012, PMID 22450305;
- renewal review PMID 28608584.

Central finding:

```text
old learning
+
new competing/context-sensitive learning
```

is often a better model than:

```text
old learning erased
```

## 4.2 Consequences for SP

This yields several important boundaries:

1. A return of the old continuation is not automatically regression to zero.
2. Training only in one safe context can create context-bound performance.
3. Old cue combinations may remain capable of retrieving the old response.
4. Re-entry after lapse is part of the learning architecture, not an exceptional failure mode.
5. `I chose differently once` is very weak evidence that the automatic route has changed.

# 5. How does a new continuation become automatic?

## 5.1 Repetition + stable cue/context

**Evidence status:** strong conceptual support; intervention estimates vary.

Habit research describes habits as increasingly cue-dependent responses formed through repetition in recurring contexts (Wood & Rünger, PMID 26361052; Gardner-related reviews; cue-contingent automaticity PMID 20658824).

Recent objective work also shows that increased practice strengthens cue-response associations that support performance under cognitive load (PMID 38047612).

Working relation:

```text
relevant cue/context
→ repeated same useful response
→ stronger retrieval/accessibility
→ lower deliberative demand
```

## 5.2 Time is not a fixed threshold

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of health behavior habit formation (PMID 39685110) found large individual variability. Reported central times were measured in months/weeks rather than supporting a universal `21-day` rule.

SP must therefore not define a fixed automation threshold at this stage.

## 5.3 Skill automaticity and reduced cognitive load

Skill research shows repeated practice can increase speed, reduce cognitive load and produce automaticity (e.g. PMID 30944847; cognitive skill review PMID 15012487).

The habit/skill distinction matters:

- some automaticity is efficient skilled execution;
- some automaticity can reduce flexibility;
- deliberate practice can involve repeatedly breaking and restructuring suboptimal automatic components (PMID 35307293).

This is highly compatible with the Owner's stated long-term direction.

# 6. Effective automaticity vs rigid automaticity

The project should not equate `more automatic` with `better`.

Candidate distinction:

## Rigid automaticity

```text
cue
→ default response
→ continues despite relevant change in reality/outcome
```

## Effective automaticity

```text
familiar relevant cue
→ efficient trained response
while
meaningful mismatch / new fact can still reopen participation
```

This is a **SP design hypothesis**.

It suggests that mature automaticity should combine:

```text
efficiency in familiar conditions
+
sensitivity to relevant change
+
recoverability when the old response becomes misaligned
```

Expert-decision literature is compatible with fast recognition-based decisions in trained professionals under time pressure, while analytical processing remains available in less familiar/uncertain situations (systematic review PMID 37823061).

Bill Williams' `Trading Chaos` contains a related practitioner intuition: extensive attention to the present market/process and repeated use of a system are intended to reduce unnecessary deliberation. This is useful as conceptual ancestry, not scientific validation.

# 7. Context stability vs generalization — an important training tension

A new habit forms more readily when cue/context relationships are stable.

But behavior trained only in one context may remain context-bound.

Learning literature on variability/generalization suggests that more varied practice can improve transfer, although it may slow initial acquisition (e.g. PMID 35577719; motor-transfer meta-analysis PMID 39205981).

Therefore the likely architecture is **phased**, not contradictory:

```text
PHASE 1 — acquisition
stable, recognizable cue
+ simple repeatable alternative

PHASE 2 — strengthening
repetition + feedback

PHASE 3 — variation / transfer
same functional operation across varied contexts/states

PHASE 4 — recovery
practice rapid re-entry when old response reappears
```

Only Phase 1 belongs clearly inside early Stage 1 training. Robust transfer under high load approaches later-stage work.

---

# 8. Stress/load boundary

Stress research indicates that stress can shift instrumental control toward more habitual responding in some conditions (review PMID 21219935).

SP implication:

A continuation that is available in calm reflection may not be available under the original load.

This supports three design boundaries:

1. do not infer mastery from calm retrospective performance;
2. reduce friction for the new response;
3. later training must test retrieval under representative load.

But robust performance under substantial stress belongs primarily to later stages; Stage 1 must not require Stage 4 capacity.

# 9. Proposed SP Automaticity Transformation Loop

**Status:** HYPOTHESIS for design/research.

```text
1. OLD ROUTE IS CUED
context / state / event
→ familiar continuation becomes highly available

2. INTERRUPTION WINDOW
self-detection / external cue / changed context / prepared if-then trigger
→ old continuation is delayed, stopped, reduced or made non-exclusive

3. OPEN EDGE
what has already happened is separated from what remains influenceable

4. PREPARED / AVAILABLE ALTERNATIVE
one executable continuation is available now

5. REALIZATION
alternative is actually performed

6. CONSEQUENCE / FEEDBACK
what happened next becomes current-position data

7. REPETITION
when the relevant cue recurs, the useful response is repeated

8. RE-AUTOMATIZATION
alternative becomes easier/faster/more default in that context

9. VARIATION / TRANSFER
training occurs across relevant contexts/states

10. RECOVERY
old route reappears → re-entry without treating lapse as total reset
```

This is not a claim that all ten steps occur consciously.

The intended endpoint is that steps 2–5 can themselves become increasingly fast and low-load.

# 10. Stage 1 implications

The research suggests that Stage 1 Learning Units should probably train **not only detection and choice**, but a minimal `interruption → replacement → realization` chain.

Potential Stage 1 learner-facing requirements:

### A. Detect a useful early-enough cue
Not necessarily the original trigger; any point before the relevant next continuation closes.

### B. Create a micro-interruption
Pause, stop, context shift, retrieval cue or other brief change that prevents immediate continuation from remaining exclusive.

### C. Have one executable alternative ready
The alternative should be concrete enough to act on under the current state.

### D. Actually realize it
RC-018 remains binding.

### E. Repeat when the relevant cue returns
The aim is not endless conscious analysis but increased accessibility of the new route.

This is still a hypothesis and must be tested against learner load and Stage 1 boundaries.

# 11. Possible hierarchy of intervention leverage

**HYPOTHESIS — not an efficacy ranking.**

A useful design order may be:

```text
1. change context/cue before the old route fires
2. place retrieval cue / if-then alternative at the critical point
3. use brief interruption or competing response when route begins
4. execute substitute continuation
5. reinforce/repeat it in the same relevant context
6. later vary context to build transfer
7. train recovery after lapse
```

This order shifts the method away from heroic willpower at peak impulse.

# 12. Strongest Red Team attacks

## RT-ATR-01 — `habit` may be the wrong category

Not every SP automatic episode is a habit. Some are emotion-linked actions, practiced skills, social scripts, state effects, deliberate shortcuts or one-off reactions.

**Constraint:** use `automatic continuation` as the project-level umbrella. Apply habit evidence only where cue/repetition structure actually fits.

## RT-ATR-02 — interruption can itself impair skilled automaticity

In well-trained skilled behavior, conscious intervention can sometimes degrade performance. Therefore SP must not teach interruption whenever behavior is automatic.

**Constraint:** interruption is relevant only when current facts/direction signal meaningful mismatch, uncertainty or changed conditions.

## RT-ATR-03 — environment design can replace capability

If all success depends on removing cues or external reminders, the learner may not have developed portable participation.

**Constraint:** track scaffold/context dependence separately. External supports are valid, but they must not be mislabeled independent capacity.

## RT-ATR-04 — stable-context training may create brittle new habit

A replacement trained in one context can fail elsewhere.

**Constraint:** later transfer training is required; early Stage 1 cannot claim generalized automation.

## RT-ATR-05 — replacement can preserve the same harmful function

A substitute action may merely move the cost elsewhere.

**Constraint:** consequence/next-position feedback remains required even though hidden-cause analysis is not.

## RT-ATR-06 — intentional stopping can become control obsession

Training inhibition at every impulse could produce constant self-monitoring.

**Constraint:** automaticity remains neutral; no interruption is rewarded merely because it is conscious.

## RT-ATR-07 — clinical evidence cannot be generalized mechanically

HRT, addiction, eating and smoking studies address specific populations/behaviors.

**Constraint:** they contribute candidate mechanisms/intervention classes, not universal SP protocols.

## RT-ATR-08 — effective automaticity may need two kinds of automation

The eventual target may not only be `new behavior becomes habit` but also:

```text
mismatch detection becomes faster
+
useful response becomes faster
```

Whether `detection of mismatch` itself can/should become automatic is an OPEN research question.

# 13. Open questions

**OPEN-1:** What is the smallest reliable `micro-interruption` that can be trained without adding excessive cognitive load?

**OPEN-2:** Does the new response need to be a direct competing response, or is any direction-compatible first step sufficient?

**OPEN-3:** Which cue should be trained: external context, internal state, urge, first action fragment, consequence, or a system prompt?

**OPEN-4:** Can SP distinguish empirically between `old route suppressed` and `new route becoming default` using pilot data?

**OPEN-5:** How should prompt exposure be tapered to test independent retrieval without prematurely removing useful scaffolding?

**OPEN-6:** How many contexts/states are necessary before claiming transfer? No threshold is currently justified.

**OPEN-7:** What should count as evidence of `effective automaticity`: speed, reduced cognitive load, cue-linked realization, preserved flexibility, recovery after mismatch, or a combination?

**OPEN-8:** Can detection of mismatch become a trained automatic sentinel without producing hypervigilance?

# 14. Gate D recommendation

Before finalizing Learning Units, Gate D should integrate a new central distinction:

```text
Stage 1 is not only:
NOTICE → CHOOSE → DO

It is likely:
NOTICE / CUE
→ INTERRUPT ENOUGH
→ MAKE ONE ALTERNATIVE AVAILABLE
→ REALIZE
→ REPEAT
```

with the long-term training trajectory:

```text
old automaticity
→ selection/interruption window
→ repeated reality-aligned realization
→ new/effective automaticity
```
